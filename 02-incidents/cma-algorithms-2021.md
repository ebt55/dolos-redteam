# UK CMA, "Algorithms" (2021) — the regulator builds an audit capability

## Date
Published **19 January 2021**; call for information closed **16 March 2021**.

## Actors
The CMA's Data, Technology and Analytics (DaTA) Unit — a multidisciplinary team of about 35 data scientists, data engineers, technologists and behavioural scientists — publishing in support of the CMA's **Analysing Algorithms Programme**. A launch panel on 25 January 2021 featured Ariel Ezrachi, Bill Kovacic, Cathy O'Neil and Christo Wilson, chaired by Stefan Hunt.

## What happened
The CMA published a research paper identifying ways algorithms can harm competition and consumers, and used it to launch a programme of work. The harms it names are three: **direct consumer harms** (personalised pricing and other online choice architecture), **exclusion of competitors** (e.g. self-preferencing), and the **potential for algorithms to facilitate collusion**. The paper also summarises "techniques that could be used to analyse algorithmic systems" and discusses the regulator's role in addressing these harms. The programme's stated aims: identify problematic markets and firms, take cases forward, and **work with others to set standards and determine how algorithms should be audited**.

## Coordination mechanism
The paper's subject matter is algorithmic coordination broadly — pricing algorithms used to facilitate explicit cartels, hub-and-spoke arrangements, and tacit/emergent coordination. It is a taxonomy-and-capability document, not an investigation of a specific mechanism.

## Legal status
**Soft law and capacity-building, not enforcement.** No firm was accused, no infringement found. The CMA's approach is to apply existing UK competition law (the Chapter I/II prohibitions) to algorithmic conduct and to build the analytical toolkit to do so. Unlike California's AB 325, the UK has created no algorithm-specific offence — that is my characterisation of the UK regime, not a claim made on the CMA page.

## Why it matters for AI-agent collusion
- **The institutional analogue of sprint track 2.** The CMA's programme is the closest real-world example of an organisation deciding that auditing algorithms requires new *methods* (not just new *rules*), and funding a unit to build them. That is what a collusion-audit project is: a method looking for an institution.
- **Audit standards are the missing infrastructure.** The paper's stated ambition — "determine how algorithms should be audited" — is precisely sprint Q2.3 ("what should count as convincing evidence?") posed by a regulator rather than a court. Courts need a case; regulators need a repeatable test.
- **The limits are instructive.** A 2021 capability paper did not produce, by itself, a single algorithmic-collusion enforcement action I could verify. Capability without a legal hook or a live case is not enforcement. Sprint teams should be honest about the same gap: a detector is not a finding.
- **A comparative note worth one line in a write-up.** The UK pathway is ex-ante/standards-based; the US pathway is adversarial and case-by-case (*RealPage*, *Gibson*); California's AB 325 is statutory and lowers the pleading bar. The same technical evidence would fare very differently in each forum — which is itself an argument for reporting evidence in graded, jurisdiction-aware terms.
