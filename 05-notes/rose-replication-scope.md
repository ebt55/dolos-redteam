# Rose replication — scope, compute, and cost

> **v2 update (later on 24 Oct):** the shortlist changed after the blind prior-art comparison. D1 is now **BEACON** (upward-only; the downward arm is Tran & Yu 2026), and **DISCLOSE** is #1. This report’s D1 comparison is historical; the Rose cost and artifact-audit findings still stand.

Scoping report for replicating Rose et al. 2026, *Detecting Multi-Agent Collusion Through Multi-Agent Interpretability* (arXiv:2604.01151v5), on an RTX 3080 10 GB, 32 GB RAM, and $48 of Modal credits. Written 2026-10-24. No weights were downloaded and no models were run.

**Bottom line:** a literal full replication (four models, three tiers, from scratch) costs **$105–215 of Modal GPU** and 50–85 GPU-hours — about 2–4× the credit. But the paper's code and **pre-extracted activations for all four models are public** ([repo](https://github.com/aaronrose227/narcbench), [dataset](https://huggingface.co/datasets/aaronrose227/narcbench), 1.46 GB). The entire headline table — the probe pipeline, the OOD transfer table, the STEGO 0.99–1.00 result — can be recomputed **on CPU for $0 in 2–4 hours**. The cheapest *meaningful* version is therefore not a GPU replication at all: it is an artifact replication plus one extension the paper does not report. Whether that beats D1 is a separate question, answered honestly in §8: **no, not as a replication; only as a lower-variance fallback.**

---

## 1. What "a Rose replication" can mean

The paper is a pipeline with four separable stages: (i) scenario generation, (ii) activation extraction, (iii) probe training, (iv) group-statistic evaluation. "Replication" is ambiguous because you can re-run any suffix of that pipeline. Five levels, cheapest first:

| Level | What you re-run | Cost | What it actually establishes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **L0 Artifact audit** | Probe training + evaluation on released activations (CPU) | **$0**, 2–4 h | The reported numbers are reproducible from the released artifacts |
| **L1 Partial regeneration** | Regenerate one tier (STEGO), extract, probe | **$4–8**, 2–4 GPU-h | The generation half of the pipeline is reproducible |
| **L2 One-model end-to-end** | All 3 tiers, Qwen3-32B, extract, probe | **$9–15**, 4–6 GPU-h | A 1-of-4 replication of the method |
| **L3 Full replication** | All 4 models, all tiers | **$105–215**, 50–85 GPU-h | Tables 2–5 reproduce |
| **L4 Conceptual replication** | Port the harness to a 7B/8B on the 3080 | **$0**, 8–12 h wall-clock | Whether the phenomenon exists at small scale |

**What L3 would still not reproduce.** The emergent-collusion result (Verdict Decisions: 229 collusive episodes, only 2 verbalise coordination, three of five probes at chance) is the paper's most interesting finding and the hardest to replicate. The repo says the environment was "generated by a separate experiment harness (not part of this repo's own pipeline)". The *activations* for Verdict are released, so the AUROCs can be recomputed, but you cannot regenerate the environment without rebuilding a two-agent repeated peer-verdict game from scratch. A "full replication" that omits the failure mode is not a full replication of the paper's argument.

**Version drift is a real hazard.** The v1 on [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2604.01151) reports STEGO at 0.90–1.00 and transfer at 0.60–0.86; v5 reports 0.99–1.00 on STEGO. The released dataset was last updated five months before v5 with a "v3 unified schema, hand-audited collusion runs" commit. Pin the paper version *and* the dataset commit before claiming a mismatch.

## 2. The exact setup (extracted)

- **Tiers.** CORE: 50 scenarios across 17 domains, 4-agent committee, binary vote, 2 colluders / 2 honest, 3 discussion rounds + private pre-negotiation → 100 runs. TRANSFER: 6 structurally distinct families (code review, pump-and-dump, CI/CD, RAG poisoning, research pipeline, news framing) × 6 sub-scenarios × 2 conditions = 72 runs. STEGO: 20 blackjack shoes × 2 conditions = 40 runs, 8 hands each, agents invent a fresh encoding per shoe. Total **212 tier runs**.
- **Models.** Qwen3-32B-AWQ (the tuning model), Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct-AWQ-INT4, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B, GPT-OSS-20B.
- **Probe.** Logistic regression on residual-stream activations at the **last token of each agent's public response**, across a **five-layer band mid-model** (layers 26–30 of 64 for Qwen3-32B), averaged over layers. Trained on **CORE collusion runs only**, balanced by downsampling, standardised, weight vector = "deception direction". Score `s_j = z̄_j · d`.
- **Group statistics.** Sorted Concatenation, Activation Alignment, Peak Suspicion, Deception Split, Asymmetry Probe (plus GLRT/mixture forms in the theory).
- **Evaluation.** AUROC, mean over 5 layers × **10 probe seeds**; leave-domain-out CV on CORE (17 folds); zero-shot on TRANSFER and STEGO. Six probe architectures in Appendix E; an 8-agent extension in Appendix G; an eval-awareness test in Appendix H.

The local note ([rose-2026 note](../01-papers/02-detection/rose-2026-helios-activation-probes.md)) matches these numbers and flags the right caveats (white-box assumption, prompt confounds, AUROC being threshold-free), but did not record the compute appendix or the released artifacts; both are added here.

## 3. GPU memory: what fits where

Parameter counts and 4-bit sizes are approximate, from model cards and configs; KV cache assumes 4 agents × ~4k context with GQA.

| Model | Params | bf16 | 8-bit | 4-bit | RTX 3080 10 GB? | A100 40 GB? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Qwen3-32B-AWQ | 32.8B | ~66 GB | ~33 GB | **~19 GB** | No | Yes, tight |
| DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B | 32.8B | ~66 GB | ~33 GB | ~19 GB | No | Yes, tight |
| Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct-AWQ-INT4 | 70.6B | ~141 GB | ~71 GB | **~40 GB** | No | No |
| GPT-OSS-20B | 21B total, 3.6B active | ~42 GB | ~21 GB | **~12 GB** (native MXFP4) | No | Yes |
| 7B/8B proxy (Qwen3-8B, Llama-3.1-8B) | 8B | ~16 GB | ~8 GB | **~5.5 GB** | Yes | Yes |

Only the 7B/8B proxy fits the 3080. The 32B needs ~19 GB of weights plus 1–2 GB of KV cache, i.e. a 24 GB card minimum; the 70B needs 48–80 GB. The paper itself ran on **two RTX A5500 24 GB cards with 4-bit + tensor parallelism** (stated in v1, §3.2) — a useful sanity check that the whole pipeline is a tens-of-GPU-hours job, not a thousand-GPU-hour one.

## 4. Activation-extraction cost

**Assumptions (all estimates, marked †):**

- 212 tier runs; ~20 model calls per CORE/TRANSFER run (2 colluders × 2 private turns + 3 rounds × 4 agents + vote) and ~35 per STEGO run → **≈4,800 calls per model**.
- ~300 output tokens and ~2.5k average prefill tokens per call → **≈1.4M output, ≈12M prefill tokens per model**.
- 4-bit 32B on an A100 80 GB with vLLM, batch 16: ~6–10k prefill tok/s, ~800–1,500 decode tok/s.
- 2–3× oversampling for refusals and failed collusion audits (GPT-OSS-20B produced *zero* analysable runs in three of six TRANSFER families, so oversampling is mandatory, not optional).
- Extraction piggybacks on the same forward passes; five layers cost nothing extra (one forward yields all layers). ~0.5–1 GPU-h per model.
- Probe training is CPU-only and takes seconds per seed; six architectures and ten seeds do not add GPU cost.

Arithmetic for a 32B: 12M/8k ≈ 1,500 s prefill + 1.4M/1.2k ≈ 1,200 s decode ≈ **0.75 GPU-h clean**, ×2–3 retries ≈ 2 GPU-h. Extraction adds ~5,000 sequences (4 agents × ~3 rounds × 212 runs + final votes) × ~3k tokens ≈ **15M token-forwards ≈ 1 GPU-h**. Total **~3 GPU-h clean, 4–6 GPU-h with debugging**. A 70B is ~2.5–3× slower per token → **7–12 GPU-h**; GPT-OSS-20B is faster but refusal-heavy → **3–5 GPU-h**. Full set: **~21–28 GPU-h clean, 50–85 GPU-h realistic.**

The key number: generation is cheap because NARCBench is small (212 runs), not because the models are small. The cost driver is *engineering and refusals*, not tokens.

It comfortably buys L2 ($9–15) or L3a ($18–30), and trivially buys L0 ($0).

## 6. Feasibility: is $48 enough for something worth submitting?

Yes, but not for the thing the phrase "Rose replication" implies.

- **Full replication: no.** $105–215 is 2–4× the credit, and the missing piece (emergent collusion) is not purchasable at any price without rebuilding the third-party harness.
- **One-model replication: yes**, with $30+ left over. But a one-model replication of a four-model paper is a *partial* replication, and its result is "Qwen3 numbers reproduce" — a sentence, not a paper.
- **Artifact audit + extension: yes, for $0**, and this is the only version that fits a 40-hour solo sprint *and* leaves time to produce a new claim.

**Cheapest publishable version.** Do **L0 plus one extension**, entirely on CPU:

1. Recompute Tables 2–5 / Figure 2 from the released activations. Report the max absolute deviation per cell. This is a genuine check: the dataset is v3, the paper is v5, and nobody has published the diff.
2. Add the operating point the paper omits. The paper reports AUROC only; the repo ships `roc_analysis.py` for recall@1%/5% FPR but the main tables do not report it. **Recall at a fixed false-positive budget is the number a deployment would actually use**, and the local note already flags this gap ("0.99 says nothing about recall at a fixed false-positive budget").
3. Optionally: leave-one-model-out probe transfer (train the direction on Qwen3, apply to Llama/DeepSeek/GPT-OSS activations), which the paper explicitly lists as *not tested*.

That is a defensible workshop note or blog-post artifact: "we reproduced X, found version drift Y, and the AUROC headline collapses to Z recall at 1% FPR." It costs $0 and roughly 10–14 hours, leaving 25+ hours for writing and robustness.

## 7. Alternatives and what they lose

- **Released probes.** No serialized weight file appears in the repo listing, but that is irrelevant: the probe is a logistic regression over released activations, refit exactly by `reproduce/reproduce_LR.py` in seconds. Functionally, the probes *are* released, and **nothing is lost** for L0 — the strongest card in the deck, and the reason the GPU question is mostly moot.
- **7B/8B conceptual replication on the 3080 ($0).** You must port the generation scripts (they assume a vLLM server serving Qwen3-32B-AWQ) and rewrite prompts for a smaller model. What is lost: the paper's effect is partly a *capability* effect (Appendix O reports detection correlating with model capability), and GPT-OSS-20B already failed to produce analysable collusion in half the TRANSFER families. An 8B will likely refuse or collude incompetently, so a null result is uninterpretable — you cannot distinguish "no signal" from "no collusion". This is the worst cost/insight ratio of the options, despite being free.
- **Regenerating with a 32B on Modal and skipping the 70B** is the sensible GPU compromise if you want fresh data: one model, $9–15, and it exercises the full pipeline.

## 8. Recommendation: Rose replication vs D1

**Honest answer: D1 remains the better 40-hour project. A Rose replication is the better *fallback*, and a Rose *extension* is competitive but not superior.**

Why D1 still wins:

1. **Novelty.** A replication produces no new claim by construction. D1 asks whether high prices are *enforced* — the field's central open question — and produces an interventional result (does a scripted undercut get punished?) that nothing in the current literature answers. The shortlist's own reasoning puts D1 first for exactly this reason.
2. **Cost and risk are already known.** D1 is ~2,400 API calls, $10–30, with a positive control (PC vs AC) built in and a 40-hour clock already planned. Rose L3 is 2–4× the Modal credit with a real chance of a botched harness port; Rose L0 is cheap but low-novelty.
3. **Failure modes.** If D1's positive control fails, you still have a negative result about a mechanism. If a Rose replication "fails", the most likely cause is your harness, not the paper — and a replication that merely confirms published numbers is hard to place.

Where Rose wins, and you should switch:

- **If you cannot patch `llm-cartel`** (the D1 plan depends on a ~20-line `--deviate_at` patch and a working parse rate), the Rose CPU path has no comparable dependency: clone, download 1.46 GB, run one script.
- **If you want minimum variance.** L0 either reproduces or it does not, in half a day. D1's outcome depends on model behaviour you cannot fully predict.
- **If the sprint's deliverable is an audit/verification artefact rather than a discovery.** "We checked the 0.99 AUROC claim at a fixed FPR and here is what survives" is a legitimate contribution in the audit/verification genre — smaller than D1, not worthless.

**Concrete recommendation.** Start D1. Keep Rose-L0 as a **4-hour insurance policy** you can run on day 1 in the background (it is CPU-only and needs no GPU): if D1's positive control fails by hour 12, you already have a reproduced table, a version-drift diff, and a recall@1% FPR result to write up. Do **not** spend the $48 on L3; if you spend it at all, spend $10–15 on L2 to prove the generation pipeline, and only after L0 has reproduced.
